A reader's guide

Two explainers look at the same recording

Two programs guess the emotion in Arabic speech. Both are often right. The question is not which is more accurate — it is whether they point at the same part of the sound as their reason.

01 · The question

Two doctors, one patient

Two doctors examine the same patient. Both conclude “this person has a fever.” Asked why, the first says “their temperature is 39 degrees,” the second says “their face is flushed and they are shivering.” Same conclusion; different evidence.

This paper runs that test on machines. If two equally accurate AI systems point at different things, then the explanation a system gives may say more about how it was built than about the speech itself.

02 · The material

Two ways of describing the same sound

Four Arabic corpora — SAUDI, YSED, ERDMA, KEDAS — supply 1,000 locked-test recordings that were never used for training. Each recording goes down two paths at once.

Path A

Summary numbers

88 eGeMAPS numbers → CatBoost → TreeSHAP

The recording is reduced to 88 measurements: average pitch, loudness, breathiness. TreeSHAP then answers which property pushed hardest.

This summary throws away when anything happened.

Path B

The raw waveform

Raw waveform → XLS-R 300M → Integrated Gradients

The recording goes in as it is, 16,000 numbers per second. Integrated Gradients answers which moments pushed hardest.

This map does not know which acoustic property lives at those moments.

Here is the difficulty: path A answers which property, path B answers when. They cannot be compared directly — so the paper builds a bridge.

04 · The instrument

How much agreement does pure chance produce?

The two sides agree on 197 of 996 recordings — 19.78%. That number alone means nothing. Two labellers with no relationship at all still agree fairly often, simply out of habit.

06 · The scale

Perfect agreement was never possible

The two sides have different labelling habits: TreeSHAP favours spectral change, IG favours pitch movement. When the two sets of habits differ, some recordings simply cannot match, no matter how good the method. The highest attainable agreement is fixed by those habits alone.

07 · The second finding

Why the IG maps were broken

Integrated Gradients carries a guarantee called completeness: add up all the attributions and the total must equal the difference in score between the real recording and silence. If it does not, the map is not actually accounting for the model's decision.

IG works by fading the recording up from silence, like turning a volume dial from 0 to 1, recording the model's sensitivity along the way. A computer can only sample a finite number of points. The problem: inside XLS-R, almost all of the score change happens at the very start.

08 · Why this null is trustworthy

The instrument was shown to work first

Saying “we found nothing” is cheap. Maybe there is nothing; maybe the instrument is blunt. So the identical procedure was run on cases that should show agreement.

The first three rows compare two views of one representation — the agreement is real there, and the procedure finds it easily. The last row compares the two different representations. So the absence is a property of the data, not of the test.

09 · The limits

What the paper does not claim

  • Not that the two models use different acoustic mechanisms. What is compared are concept labels under one researcher-designed mapping.
  • Not that either explanation method is more correct. There is no ground truth to compare against.
  • Not anything about unseen speakers. The data is split by utterance, not by speaker.
  • Not that differences between corpora are caused by dialect. The four also differ in recording conditions, speakers and protocol.
  • Not that IG or SHAP are untrustworthy in general. One way of running IG failed its own requirement, and that can be fixed.
10 · Glossary

Terms that appear in the paper

eGeMAPS

A standard list of 88 numbers summarising one recording: average pitch, loudness, breathiness, voiced segments per second. If a recording were a face, these would be height, weight and head circumference.

Frozen encoder + linear probe

XLS-R's parameters are frozen, never modified. Only a small layer at the end is trained. The consequence: the explanations describe that small layer, not XLS-R in general.

SHAP and TreeSHAP

From game theory: how to divide a team's winnings fairly, by averaging how much the prize grows when each member joins, across every possible joining order. Here the team members are the 88 descriptors. TreeSHAP is an exact shortcut for tree models.

Integrated Gradients

Start from silence, fade up to the real recording, and record the model's sensitivity at each moment along the way. Add it all up.

Completeness

The guarantee that all IG attributions must sum exactly to the score difference. If they do not, part of the decision is unexplained by anything in the map.

Permutation test and the null hypothesis

Scramble the data on purpose, many times, and see what values chance throws up. That is the reference for “mere chance.” Section 04 runs one live.

p value

The probability of a result at least this extreme if there were no relationship at all. Here 0.9586 — highly consistent with chance.

Minimum detectable effect

The smallest effect this design can catch. Here 2.7 percentage points. It turns “we found nothing” into “anything above 2.7 points would have been seen, and nothing was.” Section 05 demonstrates it.

Positive control

A test that must come out positive. Before concluding “there is no gold in this river,” check that your detector beeps against a gold ring.

Attainable ceiling

The highest agreement possible given the two sides' label frequencies. Section 06 computes it from the data: 43.67%, not 100%.
